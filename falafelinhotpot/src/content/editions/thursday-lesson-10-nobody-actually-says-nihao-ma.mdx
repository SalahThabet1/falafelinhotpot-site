## How Chinese people actually say hi

If you've studied Chinese from a textbook, you probably learned <Zh>你好</Zh> (nǐ hǎo) for hello, <Zh>你好吗</Zh> (nǐ hǎo ma) for "how are you," and <Zh>很高兴认识你</Zh> (hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ) for "nice to meet you." All technically correct. All things a real Chinese person will understand perfectly.

Almost nobody actually talks like this.

Here's what real greetings sound like — the ones you'll actually hear on the street, in group chats, and in meetings.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"吃了吗？"} transliteration={"chī le ma?"} gloss={"Have you eaten?"} />

Forget "how are you" — ask if they've eaten. 吃了吗？ exists, but it sounds like something out of a phrasebook. What people actually say, especially between neighbors, older generations, or anyone catching up casually, is this.

It's not really about food. It's a stand-in for "how are you," rooted in a culture where checking on someone's wellbeing has historically meant checking whether they have enough to eat. You don't need a real answer — <Zh>吃了</Zh> (chī le) — "yeah, I ate" — is basically just "I'm good, thanks."

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"最近怎么样？"} transliteration={"zuìjìn zěnmeyàng?"} gloss={"How have you been lately?"} />

Catching up with someone? Skip "how are you" entirely. <Zh>最近怎么样？</Zh> is the one you use with someone you haven't seen in a while — far more natural than <Zh>你好吗</Zh> (nǐ hǎo ma), which barely gets used in real conversation at all.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"干嘛呢？"} transliteration={"gànmá ne?"} gloss={"What are you up to?"} />

Texting a friend? There's a phrase for that too. <Zh>干嘛呢？</Zh> is casual, texting-register Chinese — the equivalent of "what's up" between friends, not something you'd say to your boss.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"幸会"} transliteration={"xìnghuì"} gloss={"Literally \"fortunate meeting\""} />

And the one that inspired this whole newsletter. You learned <Zh>很高兴认识你</Zh> (hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ) for "nice to meet you" — perfectly correct, a little formal, a little textbook. In real business or polished social settings, native speakers often reach for something with more personality.

Often doubled for warmth — <Zh>幸会幸会</Zh> (xìnghuì xìnghuì) — it's the kind of phrase that immediately signals you're not just translating in your head.

<PullQuote>It's a level up, not a replacement: 很高兴认识你 isn't wrong. 幸会 is what confident, fluent speakers actually reach for.</PullQuote>
