## The grand opener

<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/thursday-lesson/thursday-lesson-06-we-are-learning-chinese-pickup-lines-today-fig-6.jpg" alt="A bold romantic gesture on a Ming dynasty street" />

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"你是我见过最好看的人"} transliteration={"nǐ shì wǒ jiànguò zuì hǎokàn de rén"} gloss={"You are the most beautiful person I have ever seen."} />

Bold. Direct. Slightly unhinged. This one bypasses the entire indirect Chinese communication style and goes straight for it. Either they laugh and like you more, or they walk away. No middle ground.

<PullQuote><Zh>说真的，你是我见过最好看的人。</Zh><br /><Py>Shuō zhēn de — Honestly, you are the most beautiful person I have ever seen.</Py></PullQuote>


## The poetic one

<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/thursday-lesson/thursday-lesson-06-we-are-learning-chinese-pickup-lines-today-fig-1.jpg" alt="Eyes that speak without words — Tang dynasty romantic poetry energy" />

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"你的眼睛会说话"} transliteration={"nǐ de yǎnjīng huì shuōhuà"} gloss={"Your eyes can speak."} />

This is peak Chinese romantic poetry energy. Telling someone their eyes have a language of their own. Wildly effective on anyone who appreciates a line that sounds like it came from a Tang dynasty love poem. Which, in China, is a lot of people.

<PullQuote><Zh>你的眼睛会说话，我都听见了。</Zh><br /><Py>Nǐ de yǎnjīng huì shuōhuà, wǒ dōu tīngjiàn le. — Your eyes can speak — and I heard everything.</Py></PullQuote>


## The sunshine line

<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/thursday-lesson/thursday-lesson-06-we-are-learning-chinese-pickup-lines-today-fig-2.jpg" alt="A smile like the sun — classic Chinese romance drama energy" />

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"你笑起来像太阳"} transliteration={"nǐ xiào qǐlái xiàng tàiyáng"} gloss={"When you smile, you look like the sun."} />

A classic from Chinese romance dramas — and yes, people actually say this in real life. It sounds cheesy until someone says it to you and then somehow it works completely. The sun comparison is a genuine compliment in Chinese culture — warm, bright, life-giving.

<PullQuote><Zh>你笑起来像太阳，让人暖暖的。</Zh><br /><Py>Nǐ xiào qǐlái xiàng tàiyáng, ràng rén nuǎnnuǎn de. — When you smile you look like the sun — it makes people feel warm.</Py></PullQuote>


## The audacious classic

<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/thursday-lesson/thursday-lesson-06-we-are-learning-chinese-pickup-lines-today-fig-3.jpg" alt="Lost on purpose — the oldest trick in the book" />

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"我迷路了，你能带我回家吗？"} transliteration={"wǒ mí lù le, nǐ néng dài wǒ huí jiā ma?"} gloss={"I am lost. Can you take me home?"} />

The Chinese version of a pickup line that everyone knows is a pickup line. It is so obvious that it becomes funny, and funny becomes charming. Used mostly by people who are confident enough to be openly ridiculous. Respect.

<PullQuote><Zh>我迷路了，你能带我回家吗？顺便留下你的微信。</Zh><br /><Py>I am lost — can you take me home? And leave me your WeChat while you are at it.</Py></PullQuote>


## The sneaky one

<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/thursday-lesson/thursday-lesson-06-we-are-learning-chinese-pickup-lines-today-fig-4.jpg" alt="An innocent question with hidden intent" />

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"你是哪里人？"} transliteration={"nǐ shì nǎlǐ rén?"} gloss={"Where are you from?"} />

On its own, completely innocent. But paired with "I want to visit more often" it becomes one of the smoothest indirect flirts in Mandarin. Deniable. Clever. Very Chinese. The person asking is testing the waters without ever diving in.

<PullQuote><Zh>你是哪里人？感觉我以后要常来这里了。</Zh><br /><Py>Nǐ shì nǎlǐ rén? Gǎnjué wǒ yǐhòu yào cháng lái zhèlǐ le. — Where are you from? I have a feeling I am going to be coming here a lot more.</Py></PullQuote>


## The Chinese phone number

<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/thursday-lesson/thursday-lesson-06-we-are-learning-chinese-pickup-lines-today-fig-5.jpg" alt="WeChat — where your entire life lives" />

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"你的微信是多少？"} transliteration={"nǐ de wēixìn shì duōshao?"} gloss={"What is your WeChat?"} />

In China, asking for someone's WeChat is the equivalent of asking for their number — except more intimate, because WeChat is where your entire life lives. If someone gives you their WeChat ID without hesitation, that is a very good sign. If they give you their public QR code, less so.

<PullQuote><Zh>你的微信是多少？方便加一下。</Zh><br /><Py>Nǐ de wēixìn shì duōshao? Fāngbiàn jiā yīxià. — What is your WeChat? Would love to add you — if that is okay.</Py></PullQuote>


One final thing. In Chinese, <Zh>我喜欢你</Zh> — <Py>I like you</Py> — is already a big deal. And <Zh>我爱你</Zh> — <Py>I love you</Py> — is said rarely and only when truly meant. So if you use any of the lines above and it leads somewhere real, tread carefully. Words carry weight when they are not used cheaply.
