## Five better ways to say "nice to meet you"

If you've studied Chinese from a textbook, the first sentence you probably learned was <Zh>很高兴认识你</Zh> (hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ) — "nice to meet you." Correct, understandable, and used in almost every business meeting by almost every learner.

Which is exactly the problem. Everyone learns it. Almost nobody in real business settings actually leads with it.

If you're doing business with Chinese partners, here are five phrases that'll make you sound like you actually know what you're doing — each one for a different moment, not just interchangeable filler.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"幸会"} transliteration={"xìnghuì"} gloss={"Your everyday upgrade — often doubled for warmth: 幸会幸会"} />

Your everyday upgrade. Often doubled for warmth — <Zh>幸会幸会</Zh>. Use it in any formal introduction instead of the textbook line.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"久仰"} transliteration={"jiǔyǎng"} gloss={"For meeting someone whose reputation precedes them — literally \"I've long admired you\""} />

For meeting someone whose reputation precedes them. Literally "I've long admired you." Use it when you're finally meeting someone you've heard a lot about.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"久闻大名"} transliteration={"jiǔwén dàmíng"} gloss={"Same family as 久仰, more literal: \"your name is famous\""} />

Same family as <Zh>久仰</Zh>, more literal: "your name is famous." Reserve this for someone you genuinely, deeply respect — a senior executive, a well-known figure in your industry.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"请多关照"} transliteration={"qǐng duō guānzhào"} gloss={"Not really \"nice to meet you\" at all — \"please look out for me\""} />

Not really "nice to meet you" at all — it's what you say right after the introduction, signaling "I look forward to working together, please look out for me." The phrase that opens a relationship, not just a meeting.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"请多指教"} transliteration={"qǐng duō zhǐjiào"} gloss={"A polite, humble phrase meaning roughly \"please guide me\""} />

A polite, humble phrase meaning roughly "please guide me." Perfect after introducing yourself to someone senior — shows modesty and sincerity without sounding weak.

<PullQuote>Real Chinese isn't harder than textbook Chinese. It's just different — and once you hear the difference, you can't unhear it.</PullQuote>
