## How to say "how are you" casually

<Zh>你好吗</Zh> (nǐ hǎo ma) is one of the very first phrases every learner memorizes. It's grammatically perfect, and every Chinese person will understand exactly what you mean.

**It's also almost never what people actually say to each other.** Real "how are you" in Chinese doesn't ask about your wellbeing directly at all — it asks about something specific instead.

Here's what that actually sounds like.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"吃了吗？"} transliteration={"chī le ma?"} gloss={"Literally \"have you eaten?\" — the classic, especially among older generations, neighbors, and family"} />

Literally "have you eaten?" This is the classic — especially among older generations, neighbors, and family. It's not really about food. It's a stand-in for "how are you," rooted in a culture where checking on someone meant checking whether they'd eaten. A simple <Zh>吃了</Zh> ("yeah, I ate") is basically just "I'm good."

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"去哪儿？"} transliteration={"qù nǎr?"} gloss={"Literally \"where are you headed?\" — closer to a friendly nod than a real question"} />

Literally "where are you headed?" — said in passing, often without even stopping to wait for a detailed answer. This is closer to a friendly nod than a real question. A vague <Zh>随便走走</Zh> ("just walking around") is a completely normal, honest-enough answer.

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"忙什么呢？"} transliteration={"máng shénme ne?"} gloss={"\"What are you busy with?\" — a natural opener for people you know are generally busy"} />

"What are you busy with?" A great one for people you know are generally busy — coworkers, classmates, friends mid-project. It assumes you're doing something, which is a much more natural opener than asking a blank "how are you."

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"最近怎么样？"} transliteration={"zuìjìn zěnmeyàng?"} gloss={"\"How have you been lately?\" — a real catch-up opener for someone you haven't seen in a while"} />

"How have you been lately?" This is the one you use with someone you haven't seen in a while — a real catch-up opener, far more natural than <Zh>你好吗</Zh> in that same moment.

Notice none of these actually ask "are you okay" the way English does. Chinese greetings point at something concrete — food, destination, work, time passed — and let that stand in for the whole question. That's the real pattern worth learning, more than any single phrase.

<PullQuote>The question is never really about food, or where you're going. It's just an excuse to check in.</PullQuote>
