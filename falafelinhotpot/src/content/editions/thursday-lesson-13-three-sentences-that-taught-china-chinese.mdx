Before Duolingo, there was <Zh>毛选</Zh>.

For decades, Chinese language classes — inside China and out — leaned on <Zh>毛选</Zh> (Máoxuǎn), Selected Works of Mao Zedong, for one simple reason: the sentences are short, the grammar is clean, and the rhythm sticks in your memory. You don't have to agree with the politics to notice the pattern-teaching is genuinely good.

Here are three lines almost every Chinese learner eventually meets — and the grammar hiding inside each one.

## Sentence one

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"星星之火，可以燎原。"} transliteration={"xīngxīng zhī huǒ, kěyǐ liáoyuán."} gloss={"A single spark can start a prairie fire"} />

Grammar point — <Zh>可以</Zh> (kěyǐ): possibility / capability. Small subject, big verb, no fuss.

## Sentence two

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"实事求是。"} transliteration={"shíshì qiúshì."} gloss={"Seek truth from facts"} />

Grammar point — a four-character set phrase (<Zh>成语</Zh>): two verb-object pairs stacked back to back. This exact structure shows up constantly in formal and business Chinese.

## Sentence three

<LanguageExample lang="chinese" phrase={"好好学习，天天向上。"} transliteration={"hǎohǎo xuéxí, tiāntiān xiàngshàng."} gloss={"Study well, improve every day"} />

Grammar point — reduplication: <Zh>好好</Zh> and <Zh>天天</Zh>, doubling a word to mean "thoroughly" or "every single day." One of the first patterns you'll actually use in daily speech.

These three sentences took you sixty seconds. Understanding why they're built that way — and being able to build your own — is exactly what we do, lesson by lesson.
