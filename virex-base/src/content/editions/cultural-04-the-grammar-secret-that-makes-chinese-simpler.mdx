"That must be so hard."


And I understand why. The characters look impossible. The tones sound intimidating. But here is what nobody tells you — the grammar is not what will stop you.


Compared to Arabic and English, Chinese grammar is surprisingly clean. Once you separate the writing system from the grammar, what you find underneath is genuinely simple. Let me show you exactly why.


<div class="pullquote">The characters are complex. The grammar underneath them is not.</div>


## The verb never changes form.


English has 8 forms. Arabic has more.
Chinese just says <span class="zh">去</span>.


In English, one verb takes multiple forms. I go. She goes. He went. They have gone. We were going. 
In Arabic, the verb changes based on gender, number, and person simultaneously — producing more than a dozen forms of a single verb. 

In Chinese, the verb never changes. Ever. 

<span class="zh">去</span> — <span class="py">qù</span> — to go.  
I go: <span class="zh">我去</span>。She goes: <span class="zh">她去</span>。He went: <span class="zh">他去</span>。They will go: <span class="zh">他们去</span>。

The verb is always <span class="zh">去</span>. It does not change for person, number, or gender.


## Time is expressed through particles not by changing the verb.


<EditionFigure src="/images/newsletters/cultural/cultural-04-the-grammar-secret-that-makes-chinese-simpler-fig-1.jpg" alt="Figure 1" />


<span class="zh">去</span> never changes. The particles do the work.


Chinese does express time. But it does so through small separate particles, not by modifying the verb itself.
 
<span class="zh">了</span> (le) — completed action. 
<span class="zh">我去了</span> — I went.

<span class="zh">要</span> (yào) — intention or near future. <span class="zh">我要去</span> — I am going to go.

<span class="zh">会</span> (huì) — future likelihood. <span class="zh">我会去</span> — I will go. 

<span class="zh">过</span> (guò) — past experience. <span class="zh">我去过</span> — I have been there before. 

In all four sentences, <span class="zh">去</span> stays identical. The particle does the time work — not the verb. Compare that to English where "go" becomes "went" or "have gone" and you have to memorise each form separately.


## No gendered nouns.


In Arabic, every noun is masculine or feminine — and every adjective and verb connected to it must agree. In Chinese, grammatical gender does not exist. <span class="zh">书</span> is just book. Not masculine book or feminine book.


## No irregular plurals.


In Arabic, many words have a broken plural that changes the internal structure of the entire word — each one essentially a new word to memorise. In Chinese the noun never changes for quantity. <span class="zh">书</span> means book. It also means books.


## No articles.


No "the." No "a." No "an."  
You simply say the noun. <span class="zh">猫</span> — māo.  
The cat. A cat. Cats.   
All of it is just <span class="zh">猫</span>.


The writing system is hard. The tones take practice. The vocabulary starts from zero. But none of those are grammar challenges. Most people quit before they ever get to the grammar — and miss the part that would have kept them going.


<div class="pullquote"><span class="zh">很简单</span>。Hěn jiǎndān.   
Quite simple, actually.</div>
